# A1C to estimated average glucose converter

This page converts an A1C percentage to estimated average glucose (eAG) using the ADAG equation. eAG is a population-derived estimate and is not a substitute for glucose measurements or an individual treatment target. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) describes A1C as reflecting average glucose over roughly 2–3 months. [ADA: Understanding A1C](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/a1c)

## Conversion

**eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1C (%) − 46.7.** The reverse arithmetic is **A1C (%) = (eAG + 46.7) ÷ 28.7**. For example, 7.0% gives 154 mg/dL (8.6 mmol/L) after conversion and rounding. The ADA provides this equation and conversion table, based on the ADAG study. [ADA eAG/A1C conversion](https://professional.diabetes.org/glucose_calc)

| A1C | Estimated average glucose (mg/dL) | Estimated average glucose (mmol/L) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 5.7% | 117 | 6.5 |
| 6.0% | 126 | 7.0 |
| 6.5% | 140 | 7.8 |
| 7.0% | 154 | 8.6 |
| 8.0% | 183 | 10.1 |
| 9.0% | 212 | 11.8 |
| 10.0% | 240 | 13.4 |

These are equation outputs rounded to the displayed units, not diagnostic glucose results. The formula and reference values are from the [ADA conversion resource](https://professional.diabetes.org/glucose_calc).

## Diagnostic context

For nonpregnant people, ADA criteria classify A1C 5.7–6.4% as the prediabetes range and 6.5% or higher as meeting the laboratory threshold used to diagnose diabetes. In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, ADA guidance requires confirmation with a second abnormal result. Diagnosis also depends on using an appropriate standardized laboratory test; A1C can be unsuitable when conditions alter the relationship between A1C and glucose. [ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, diagnosis and classification](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes)

These categories are screening and diagnostic criteria, not individualized risk estimates. A result below a threshold does not mean there are “no diabetes concerns,” and a calculator cannot diagnose a person.

## Limits

The conversion describes an average relationship. A personal A1C and measured glucose values can differ; ADA advises evaluation when there is substantial mismatch and notes that some conditions, including pregnancy, some hemoglobin variants, and altered red-cell turnover, affect A1C interpretation. Plasma-glucose criteria may be needed for diagnosis in those circumstances. [ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes)

Do not estimate a laboratory A1C from a set of fasting home-meter readings with the reverse equation. The equation relates A1C and average glucose in a studied population; fasting-only measurements are not the study's average-glucose input. Do not use eAG as a personal treatment target. Treatment goals are individualized; the ADA's current standards describe goals in the context of a person's health and treatment risks. [ADA Standards of Care—2026, glycemic goals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S132/163927/6-Glycemic-Goals-Hypoglycemia-and-Hyperglycemic)
