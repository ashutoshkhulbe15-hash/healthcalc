# Prodromal Labor: Stop-and-Start Contractions and When to Call

[Cleveland Clinic’s explanation of prodromal labor](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24163-prodromal-labor) describes labor-like contractions that do not progress to active labor. This page and a timer cannot determine whether that description applies to your symptoms. Contact your maternity team if you think labor has begun or are unsure, as the NHS advises below.

## Pattern alone cannot establish the stage

The [NHS description of labor](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/signs-that-labour-has-begun/) explains that the early, latent phase can take hours or days. Contractions may become longer, stronger and more frequent as labor progresses. If you think labor has started, are unsure, or have regular contractions every five minutes or more often, contact your maternity team for guidance.

Avoid using “it stopped with rest” or a particular level of pain as proof that assessment is unnecessary. Your gestational age, symptoms, history and maternity team's instructions matter.

## Do not wait for a timing rule

Contact maternity care urgently for waters breaking, vaginal bleeding, reduced baby movements, possible labor before 37 weeks, a contraction lasting over two minutes or six or more contractions in ten minutes. An imminent birth with an urge to push requires emergency help. The linked NHS guidance provides the full list and UK contact routes; elsewhere use your local maternity and emergency services.

## Useful information to report

Tell the maternity team your pregnancy week, when contractions began, their duration, the interval from one start to the next, and any fluid loss, bleeding or change in movements. Record what you observed rather than diagnosing the labor stage yourself.

The [contraction timer](/pregnancy/contraction-timer) records that timing. Its pattern flag is a communication aid, not a requirement to wait an hour before seeking help.
