# HCG Levels by Week: What Your Pregnancy Test Numbers Mean

A quantitative human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) blood test measures the concentration of a hormone produced during pregnancy. A qualitative test instead reports whether hCG is detected. Blood and urine tests are different tests; a blood concentration should not be used to predict the exact result of every home kit. [UCSF Health: Quantitative hCG testing](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

Week-by-week figures can help explain a laboratory report, but a reference interval cannot confirm pregnancy location or guarantee a healthy pregnancy. Where the location is unknown, NICE specifically says not to use serum hCG to determine location and to give clinical symptoms more importance than the blood results. [NICE NG126, section 1.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

Use this guide to understand what the number measures, how arithmetic changes are calculated and what questions to ask your care team. It does not assign a miscarriage probability, confirm twins or tell you to wait for a particular hCG level before seeking care.

**Seek urgent help for concerning symptoms.** If pregnancy is possible and you have abdominal pain or bleeding, seek medical advice even without a positive test. Sudden intense abdominal pain with marked dizziness or fainting needs emergency assessment. In the UK, NHS lists 999 or A&E for possible rupture symptoms; elsewhere use local emergency services. [NHS ectopic-pregnancy symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/ectopic-pregnancy/symptoms/). Do not wait for a second blood draw or a calculator result.

Pregnancy dating is another question. The NHS due-date calculator starts with the first day of the last period and explains that ultrasound can estimate gestational age more accurately. > **Key Takeaway:** A concentration inside a listed interval does not establish location, viability or the number of babies. Interpret the actual laboratory result with the clinical assessment; do not treat a chart as a pass/fail test.

## 📈 hCG Day by Day vs Week by Week

A week-based reference chart does not provide a validated target for each individual day. Dividing an interval into seven parts or drawing a smooth line between table entries creates a mathematical illustration, not a medical reference range. There is no “day 24” or “day 31” target calculated from this table.

Keep three kinds of timing separate: the pregnancy's estimated gestational age, the calendar date of the blood test and the elapsed hours between draws. They answer different questions. A comparison of two blood values requires the actual collection times, not just the day labels on a report.

For a **pregnancy of unknown location**, NICE advises two serum hCG measurements as close as possible to 48 hours apart, but not earlier, to guide subsequent management. That is a specific clinical pathway, not an instruction for every pregnant person to arrange repeated blood tests. Further measurements require senior clinical review in that pathway. [NICE, recommendation 1.8.5](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

A day-level calculator can describe the observed rate between two values. It cannot establish that the rate will continue, replace a follow-up scan or confirm that dating is correct. Follow your care team's test schedule rather than using online calendar predictions to decide when to seek assessment.

## 🔬 Why hCG Ranges Are So Extremely Wide

The chart itself shows substantial variation and overlap. UCSF explicitly notes differences in laboratory reference ranges, and NICE explains that last-period dating may not accurately reflect gestational age. These verified limitations are enough to show why an isolated chart comparison cannot settle the clinical question. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative); [NICE, recommendation 1.6.10](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

**Dating and ovulation timing:** Do not assume all menstrual cycles follow an identical calendar. Record the last period and any dates supplied by your fertility or pregnancy team. This guide does not assign a universal day-12-to-16 ovulation window or a fixed correction for every pregnancy.

**Implantation timing:** A week-by-week blood chart does not identify an individual's implantation date. It should not be used to reverse-calculate that date, explain away a concerning result or assign a health outcome from the timing alone.

**Variation between pregnancies:** A reference interval describes a collection of expected values, not a guarantee that two values at opposite ends represent equally healthy pregnancies. UCSF lists possible abnormal explanations for higher or lower values; an explanation still requires clinical assessment. [UCSF: What abnormal results mean](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**Laboratory context:** Keep the report's test name, units and laboratory reference interval with the number. UCSF's note about laboratory variation does not prove that every result from different laboratories is incomparable. Tell the clinical team if tests came from different services so they can decide how to interpret them.

## ⏱️ hCG Doubling Time: Why the Trend Beats Any Single Number

A trend adds information, but neither a trend nor a doubling time independently determines pregnancy location or viability. In a pregnancy of unknown location, NICE uses serial measurements to help plan management while emphasizing symptoms and ultrasound. [NICE, recommendations 1.8.2–1.8.7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

Our [hCG change calculator](/pregnancy/hcg-doubling-time-calculator) uses arithmetic:

- **Percentage change** = (second value − first value) ÷ first value × 100.
- **Calculated doubling time for an increase** = elapsed hours × ln(2) ÷ ln(second value ÷ first value).
- With equal values there is no finite doubling time. With a decrease, a halving calculation describes the mathematical rate rather than a doubling time.

These are mathematical definitions. They assume a constant exponential rate between the two measured values for the purpose of calculation; they do not demonstrate clinical validation or predict the next result.

For example, 200 to 320 mIU/mL over 48 hours is a **60% observed increase**. Its calculated doubling time is about **70.8 hours**. From 200 to 260 over 48 hours is a **30% increase**, with a calculated doubling time about **126.8 hours**. Neither example is labelled a healthy pregnancy, a miscarriage or an ectopic pregnancy.

NICE's UK pathway for pregnancy of unknown location says a rise **greater than 63% after 48 hours** suggests a developing intrauterine pregnancy, but explicitly states that ectopic pregnancy still cannot be excluded. A rise below 63%, or a fall below 50%, requires clinical review within 24 hours in that pathway. [NICE, recommendations 1.8.7–1.8.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

These are management recommendations for the specified clinical situation, not universal minimum viable rises. Do not combine thresholds from different protocols into a homemade “safe” cutoff. This guide does not retain unsupported rules giving mandatory doubling times at 1,200 or 6,000 mIU/mL or an individual outcome percentage after a slow rise.

## ⚠️ When hCG Results Are Concerning

Higher or lower results can prompt assessment, but the list of possible explanations is not a diagnosis. UCSF lists multiple pregnancy and molar pregnancy among possible explanations for higher results, and pregnancy loss or ectopic pregnancy among possible explanations for lower results relative to gestational age. [UCSF interpretation](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

In the NICE pregnancy-of-unknown-location pathway, even a decrease greater than 50% over 48 hours does not confirm that the pregnancy will not continue; the guideline says it is unlikely to continue and sets a follow-up plan. A fall below 50% or a rise below 63% calls for clinical review within 24 hours. The care team must apply the pathway to the actual situation, including boundary values and symptoms. [NICE, recommendations 1.8.8–1.8.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

A plateau is not reassurance. Neither is a rapid increase sufficient to rule out ectopic pregnancy. If symptoms change, NICE says to review the patient's condition regardless of previous results or assessments. [NICE, recommendations 1.8.3 and 1.8.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

> **Warning:** Contact your pregnancy-care team about an unexpected result and follow the agreed assessment plan. New or worsening symptoms need assessment; do not wait for the number to reach an online threshold.

## 📅 What Week Does hCG Peak?

UCSF describes a rapid rise during the first trimester followed by a slight decline. Its week-by-week intervals do not establish a single peak week or peak concentration for every pregnancy. [UCSF: Normal results](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

The chart's week-10 upper boundary is lower than its week-12 upper boundary. Reading those boundaries as one person's curve would therefore be misleading. A personal peak needs actual observations and clinical interpretation, not a curve drawn through unrelated reference limits.

A later decline can be part of the broad pattern described by the source, but this does not make every decline after week 11 reassuring. This guide does not use a concentration of 680,000 as a universal normal peak, explain a personal decline as proof of placental handover or connect changing nausea to a confirmed hCG trajectory. Ask your clinician why a particular result was measured and how it affects the assessment.

## 🕐 How Much Does hCG Rise in 12 Hours?

There is no personal 12-hour clinical target supplied here. A **hypothetical mathematical model** with a fixed 48-hour doubling time increases by 2^(12/48) − 1, or about **18.9%**, in 12 hours. A model with a fixed 72-hour doubling time gives about **12.2%**. These are derived arithmetic examples, not expected results for an individual pregnancy.

The distinction matters: selecting a doubling time for an example does not establish that the pregnancy follows that model. Nor can a calculated rate substitute for observed measurements or clinical review.

For pregnancy of unknown location, NICE specifies measurements near 48 hours apart but no earlier. That recommendation does not create a universal diagnosis from two 12-hour samples, and this guide does not advise arranging extra tests independently. [NICE, recommendation 1.8.5](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

## 👶 Twins vs Singleton: Can hCG Predict Multiples?

UCSF lists more than one fetus as a possible explanation for higher hCG. A possibility is not confirmation of twins, and the source does not establish a fixed 30–50% difference that identifies multiples. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

NHS explains that ultrasound can check whether there is more than one baby. This guide does not promise a definitive answer at a particular hCG concentration or at six to seven weeks for every scan. [NHS ultrasound scans](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/your-pregnancy-care/ultrasound-scans/).

Keep the questions separate: “Is the value higher than my laboratory's interval?” and “Has imaging identified more than one pregnancy?” The first cannot answer the second. Do not use an unusually high result as a reason to skip assessment of other possible explanations.

## 🚨 Ectopic Pregnancy and hCG

A pregnancy of unknown location may be ectopic until its location is established. NICE says serum hCG should not determine location; an empty uterus on a scan is one possible sign requiring the other scan findings, clinical presentation and hCG results to be considered together. [NICE, recommendations 1.7.3 and 1.8.1–1.8.3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

An apparently favorable rise does not clear someone of ectopic pregnancy. Conversely, a slow rise cannot identify exactly where the pregnancy is located. Neither result should be interpreted as an instruction to self-treat, stop prescribed care or wait through worsening symptoms.

NHS advises medical assessment for possible pregnancy with abdominal pain or bleeding, even without a positive test. Shoulder-tip pain needs advice right away. Sudden severe abdominal pain with dizziness or fainting can indicate rupture and needs emergency help. [NHS symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/ectopic-pregnancy/symptoms/). These instructions are based on symptoms, not on reaching an hCG cutoff.

Treatment is a specialist decision following assessment. This article does not use the hCG chart to choose methotrexate, surgery or observation. Ask the treating team what has been established, what remains uncertain and what follow-up or emergency instructions apply to you.

## 🧪 At What hCG Level Does a Home Test Turn Positive?

This guide does not assign every home test a detection threshold of 10, 20 or 25 mIU/mL. Follow the specific product's instructions. A blood concentration and the result of a urine kit are different measurements, so a blood reference table cannot guarantee the appearance of a positive line.

NHS advises that most pregnancy tests can be used from the first day of a missed period. If the next period's date is unknown, test at least **21 days after the last unprotected sex**. Some sensitive tests can be used earlier, but a negative result is less reliable than a positive one, particularly if testing too early. [NHS pregnancy testing](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/trying-for-a-baby/doing-a-pregnancy-test/).

If a result is negative and pregnancy still seems possible, NHS advises trying again after a few days and speaking to a GP if a second test is negative but the period has not arrived. Symptoms suggesting ectopic pregnancy still require assessment; a negative early test should not override that advice. [NHS testing](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/trying-for-a-baby/doing-a-pregnancy-test/); [NHS ectopic symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/ectopic-pregnancy/symptoms/).

## 🔎 At What hCG Level Is a Gestational Sac Visible?

There is no universally guaranteed sac-visibility threshold in this guide. In its UK pregnancy-of-unknown-location pathway, NICE recommends a transvaginal scan seven to fourteen days after a rise greater than 63% over 48 hours, and says to **consider an earlier scan** when serum hCG is at least **1,500 IU/L**. That is a scan-timing recommendation, not proof that an absent sac at that concentration establishes ectopic pregnancy. [NICE, recommendation 1.8.7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

For hCG concentration, **1 IU/L equals 1 mIU/mL** by unit conversion. This does not make different laboratory methods identical or turn a management threshold into a diagnostic rule.

NICE also says not to use last-period age alone to decide whether a heartbeat should be visible. Repeat-scan decisions depend on the scan type and measurements. Ask what the scan actually showed and what follow-up is needed; do not diagnose a loss or ectopic pregnancy from a blood threshold and an online image comparison. [NICE, section 1.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

## 🩺 How Your Provider Actually Uses hCG

The clinical assessment can combine symptoms, pregnancy history, serum measurements and ultrasound findings. NICE's pathway for unknown location explicitly uses hCG for planning further management, while symptoms take priority and scans assess location. [NICE, sections 1.7–1.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

Useful details to bring include the report, units, collection date and time, previous measurements, last-period date, any fertility-treatment dates and the scan report. Record symptoms and changes rather than just copying a calculated doubling time.

Ask: What is confirmed about location? What remains uncertain? What is the observed change over the actual interval? When is the next test or scan? Which symptoms mean I should contact the service or seek emergency care? These questions support the assessment without presuming that a good-looking number proves a good outcome.

## ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

**What should my hCG levels be at 3 weeks?**

UCSF lists 5–72 mIU/mL for week 3, as a source-specific blood reference interval. It is not an individual required target or a guarantee that a home test will detect pregnancy. Use the laboratory's interpretation and appropriate testing advice. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**What should my hCG levels be at 4 weeks?**

UCSF lists 10–708 mIU/mL for week 4. A value inside the interval does not confirm pregnancy health or location. Confirm the dating and laboratory context with your clinician. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative); [NICE, section 1.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

**What are normal hCG levels at 5 weeks?**

The UCSF interval is 217–8,245 mIU/mL. Other laboratory reference intervals may differ. This chart does not assign an outcome to any individual number. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**What are normal hCG levels at 7 weeks?**

UCSF lists 4,059–153,767 mIU/mL. The interval does not establish a mandatory doubling time at week 7 or rule out ectopic pregnancy. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative); [NICE, section 1.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

**What should my hCG levels be at 8 weeks?**

UCSF lists 31,366–149,094 mIU/mL. This is a reference example, not a personal target; interpret the actual report alongside the pregnancy assessment. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**What are normal hCG levels at 10 weeks?**

UCSF lists 44,186–170,409 mIU/mL. This does not establish that every pregnancy peaks in week 10 or that a result outside the interval has one particular cause. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**What are hCG levels at 12 weeks?**

UCSF lists 27,107–201,165 mIU/mL. The source describes a first-trimester rise followed by a slight decline, but the reference table does not prove that a personal decline is reassuring. [UCSF](https://www.ucsfhealth.org/care/medical-tests/hcg-blood-test-quantitative).

**My hCG did not double in 48 hours. Does this mean miscarriage?**

A doubling-time result does not diagnose miscarriage. In pregnancy of unknown location, NICE sets clinical follow-up according to the observed change, symptoms and scan findings; even a rise over 63% cannot exclude ectopic pregnancy. Contact your care team about the actual results rather than using a universal doubling rule. [NICE, section 1.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG126/chapter/diagnosis-of-viable-intrauterine-pregnancy-and-of-tubal-ectopic-pregnancy).

**Can hCG levels predict whether I am having a boy or girl?**

This guide provides no validated hCG-based sex prediction. NHS explains that finding out sex may be possible during the 20-week screening scan depending on hospital policy and the view obtained, and that the sonographer cannot be completely certain. It does not support a guaranteed answer at 16 weeks or a rule based on the hCG chart. [NHS ultrasound scans](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/your-pregnancy-care/ultrasound-scans/).
