# How Stress Affects Your Body: NIH Evidence and Its Limits

Stress can cause physical and emotional responses. NIH's NCCIH describes a short-term response involving changes in heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, muscle tension and sweating. Long-term stress **may contribute to or worsen** some health problems. It does not state that every stressed person inevitably develops organ damage. [NCCIH stress overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

This guide separates that supported explanation from the earlier article's guaranteed damage, fixed cardiovascular-risk percentage and claims of visible brain shrinkage in any individual.

## The Stress Response: What Happens in Your Body

> **Key Takeaway:** A stress response can be physical. Its presence does not determine your hormone levels, explain every symptom or diagnose disease.

NCCIH explains that stress releases hormones involved in a fight-or-flight response. Heart rate, breathing rate and blood pressure rise, muscles tense and sweating increases. Occasional stress can be a normal coping mechanism. [NCCIH introduction](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

NIMH describes stress as a physical or mental response to an external cause, which may happen once or repeatedly over time. Anxiety can occur even without a current threat. These terms are related but not interchangeable with a clinical diagnosis. [NIMH stress and anxiety definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

The former account that stress universally pauses immune surveillance, redirects digestion and produces one measurable cortisol pattern was too categorical. This article does not infer a laboratory result from how stressed you feel. A physiology explanation is not an individual test result.

## How Chronic Stress Damages Each System

**Cardiovascular system.** NCCIH describes an increase in blood pressure in the immediate stress response. That is not a verified 60% increase in future event risk for every reader, nor the equivalent of smoking five cigarettes a day. [NCCIH response description](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**Digestive system.** NCCIH says chronic stress may contribute to or worsen digestive disorders. Symptoms should not automatically be attributed to cortisol or presumed microbiome changes. [NCCIH chronic-stress paragraph](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**Sleep and headaches.** Sleep disorders and headaches are also among NCCIH's examples of problems that may be affected. The page does not establish a hormone test or prove a particular sleep disorder from stress alone. [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**Mental health.** NCCIH links stress with depression, anxiety and other mental illnesses. NIMH advises professional help when symptoms persist or coping becomes difficult. A link is not proof that stress caused an individual diagnosis. [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress); [NIMH help section](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

**Immune and other systems.** NIMH notes that anxiety which persists and interferes with life may affect several systems, including the immune system. That is not evidence that every stressful week necessarily causes infections or autoimmune disease. [NIMH distinction](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

> **Warning:** Physical symptoms deserve attention, but this article cannot identify their cause. The earlier claim that months of stress prove abnormal inflammatory markers or MRI-visible structural changes in your brain is not retained as an individual conclusion.

## Evidence-Based Stress Management

NIMH offers several coping options: journaling, relaxation or mindfulness activities, exercise, regular meals, a sleep routine, limiting excess caffeine and reaching out to supportive people. It notes that finding what works can take trial and error. It does not rank one activity as universally best or equivalent to medication without adverse effects. [NIMH coping suggestions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

NCCIH discusses evidence for relaxation, mindfulness and yoga, with differences in study quality and populations. Its safety section also notes that some people report increased anxiety or other negative experiences with relaxation practices, and that injuries can occur with yoga. These are options to discuss, not guaranteed remedies. [NCCIH evidence and safety sections](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**A practical next step:** Note the concern you want help with, when it occurs and how it affects ordinary activities. If symptoms persist or you struggle to cope, speak with a professional. This note is a communication aid, not a diagnostic monitoring scheme. [NIMH seeking help](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

Our [Perceived Stress Scale information page](/mental-health/stress-level-test) currently withholds its questionnaire, and our [burnout guide](/mental-health/burnout-quiz) does not operate a diagnostic quiz. Do not treat those pages as cortisol tests or proof of occupational disease.

For immediate distress or thoughts of self-harm in the **United States**, call or text **988**. For immediate danger, use emergency services; outside the United States, use your local services. [NIMH help and crisis information](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/so-stressed-out-fact-sheet)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How does stress affect the body physically?**

NCCIH describes changes in breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, tension and sweating during the stress response, and possible contributions to some longer-term health problems. It does not establish that your particular symptom or test result is caused by stress. [NCCIH explanation](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**Can stress make you sick?**

Long-term stress may contribute to or worsen some health problems, but that wording is more cautious than promising that stress will cause an infection or organ damage. Persistent or concerning symptoms need assessment rather than an assumed cause. [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/stress)

**Can stress cause weight gain?**

This page does not predict a change in weight from stress or diagnose high cortisol from abdominal fat. The earlier guaranteed sequence involving cravings, appetite hormones and fat storage is not retained without directly verified support for its stated scope. Discuss an unexplained weight change with your healthcare provider.
