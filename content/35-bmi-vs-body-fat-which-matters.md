# BMI and Body-Fat Percentage Measure Different Things

BMI is calculated from weight and height. The CDC describes it as a screening measure, not a direct measure of body fat: it cannot distinguish fat from lean tissue or show where fat is distributed. The CDC also cautions that BMI is one health measure and should be considered with other clinical information. [CDC BMI FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/faq/).

Body-fat percentage is intended to express fat mass as a proportion of body mass, but a number from an estimate or device is not interchangeable with BMI and does not by itself diagnose health or risk. Measurement methods differ, so the method and its limitations matter. The page's linked body-fat equation is an estimate rather than a direct measurement.

## How to interpret the comparison
Neither number alone provides a complete assessment. BMI may be affected by body composition, while a body-fat estimate does not replace clinical history, examination or laboratory findings. The CDC recommends interpreting BMI alongside other factors and notes that health professionals can assess risks related to BMI and body fat. These measures should not be used to assign a personal health status from this page.

This article makes no claim that body-fat percentage is universally more informative, more accurate or preferable to BMI. It also does not set a body-fat target or recommend a measurement frequency.
