# Can I Eat Salmon During Pregnancy? Safety, Mercury, and Benefits

Cooked salmon can be included during pregnancy. The [FDA/EPA fish chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) places salmon in its lower-mercury **Best Choices** category. Its U.S. pregnancy advice is to eat **8–12 ounces per week of a variety of lower-mercury seafood**, rather than a separate allowance for each species. Preparation matters as well: [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) recommends cooked fish instead of raw or undercooked fish and gives separate precautions for refrigerated smoked seafood.

For U.K. readers, [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) limits oily fish, including salmon, to no more than two portions a week during pregnancy. That is a separate regional recommendation. Do not combine the U.S. and U.K. limits into permission for additional salmon servings.

## How Much Salmon Can You Eat Per Week While Pregnant?

Under the [FDA/EPA chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish), the pregnancy serving guide is **4 ounces**, with **2–3 servings weekly from Best Choices**, or one serving from Good Choices. Salmon is in Best Choices. The broader weekly recommendation is 8–12 ounces of varied lower-mercury seafood. Count the fish in all your meals together, including a different fish species eaten on another day.

**Illustrative meal arithmetic:** Two hypothetical 4-ounce portions total 8 ounces; three total 12 ounces. A 4-ounce salmon portion and a 4-ounce cod portion total 8 ounces together. These calculations show how amounts add; they are not personalized meal plans or a claim that all products with those weights have identical nutrient content.

Do not add a full weekly salmon allowance to a full weekly allowance for every other fish. Likewise, a small serving does not make a fish from the chart's Avoid category equivalent to a Best Choice. Use the [FDA/EPA category chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) for the species you actually eat rather than a generic claim about “seafood.”

Fish caught by family or friends needs a further check. FDA/EPA advises consulting local advisories; if none is available, its advice is **one serving and no other fish that week**. This local-catch precaution is stated on the [fish advice page](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish), and is not overridden by recognizing the species name.

In the U.K., apply the [NHS oily-fish limit](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/): no more than two portions weekly, counting salmon with other oily fish such as trout, sardines and herring. U.S. “Best Choice” status does not erase this regional restriction. Ask your pregnancy-care professional which guidance applies if you are unsure.

## Why Salmon Is Actively Recommended During Pregnancy

The reason to consider salmon is its place among lower-mercury fish and its contribution to the diet, rather than a promise that one food changes a baby's outcome. [FDA/EPA](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) encourages lower-mercury seafood during pregnancy and lists nutrients supplied by fish, including protein, omega-3 fats, vitamin B12, vitamin D and selenium. That list describes fish as a food group; it is not a certificate of the amount in every salmon product.

Salmon supplies the omega-3 fatty acids **DHA and EPA**. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) describes DHA as an important structural component particularly abundant in the brain and retina, and provides food-composition data for several salmon preparations. A nutrient's biological role is different from evidence that a specific meal prevents a pregnancy complication or guarantees cognitive benefit.

Do not call a food's DHA content a percentage of a universal pregnancy DHA requirement. In its reference-intake discussion, [NIH](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) explains that the National Academies' omega-3 adequate intakes for people aged one year and older apply to **ALA**, and that specific reference intake recommendations were not established for EPA or DHA in that framework. A recommendation from another professional body would need to be identified separately, with its population and conditions.

Similarly, this page does not prescribe an algae or fish-oil supplement to replace salmon. Food-composition values, supplement labels and treatment doses are different information. If seafood cannot be included because of allergy, intolerance or another restriction, discuss the overall diet and any supplement decisions with the professional managing your pregnancy.

## Salmon Cooking Temperature and Safe Preparation

[CDC's pregnancy food chart](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) gives fish cooked to **145°F**, or until its flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork, as a safer choice. Raw and undercooked fish, including raw-fish sushi, sashimi and ceviche, are higher-risk choices. A lower-mercury classification does not address infection risk from raw preparation.

Use a food thermometer when checking an internal temperature. The oven setting is not the fish's internal temperature, and a recipe time alone does not measure it. The following comparisons describe what to check without prescribing an unsupported number of minutes for every fillet.

| Preparation | Check before serving |
|---|---|
| Baked or air-fried salmon | Internal fish temperature or CDC's stated opaque/flaking endpoint, rather than the appliance setting |
| Grilled or pan-cooked salmon | The fish itself meets the endpoint, not just the browned surface |
| Poached salmon | Check the fish, rather than treating hot liquid as proof it is cooked |
| Raw salmon in sushi or poke | Choose cooked fish instead under CDC pregnancy guidance |
| Refrigerated smoked salmon | Apply the separate cooked-dish precaution below |
| Salmon caught locally | Check local fish advisories in addition to cooking |

The food-safety entries follow [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html); the local-catch entry follows [FDA/EPA](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish). No cooking method in the table guarantees that an incorrectly handled or recalled product is suitable to eat.

**Smoked salmon needs a separate decision.** CDC advises avoiding refrigerated smoked seafood except in a cooked dish. It lists smoked fish cooked in a casserole or another cooked dish, shelf-stable smoked fish in sealed containers not requiring refrigeration before opening, and canned seafood as safer alternatives. A name such as “hot-smoked,” “lox” or “kippered” does not replace the storage and serving checks in the [CDC chart](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html).

U.K. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) requires cold-smoked and cured fish to be cooked until steaming hot. It also advises against raw-fish sushi even if the fish was previously frozen. Do not treat freezing or a “sushi-grade” description as permission to ignore the cited pregnancy preparation advice.

**Wild and farmed salmon:** Neither description supplies all the information needed for your meal. The [FDA/EPA chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) lists salmon without assigning a separate pregnancy allowance by farming method. [NIH](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) reports that omega-3 content varies with species, preparation and feed. It does not support a universal claim that wild salmon always contains more DHA.

## Nutritional Profile Per 3oz Serving

The following table uses the specific **3-ounce food records** in [NIH's omega-3 fact sheet, Table 2](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/). DHA and EPA are separate columns, stated in grams. These are reference food-composition values, not measurements of a particular packet or a daily supplement prescription.

| Specific salmon preparation | DHA per 3 ounces | EPA per 3 ounces |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantic salmon, farmed, cooked | 1.24 g | 0.59 g |
| Atlantic salmon, wild, cooked | 1.22 g | 0.35 g |
| Pink salmon, canned, drained | 0.63 g | 0.28 g |

**Unit conversion, derived arithmetic:** 1.24 grams is 1,240 milligrams; 0.59 grams is 590 milligrams. Adding them gives 1.83 grams, or 1,830 milligrams, of DHA plus EPA for that specific farmed Atlantic cooked-salmon record. The combined amount must not be described as DHA alone. Nor should a 3-ounce cooked-food record be silently relabelled as a different serving or preparation.

For calories, protein, sodium and other nutrients, use the actual product's label or an exact matching food record. The [FDA nutrition-label guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/whats-nutrition-facts-label) explains that labelled values generally apply to the stated serving, which is not a prescription for how much to eat. A sauce, coating or smoked preparation may require a different product entry; the table above does not analyse the entire recipe.

Avoid comparisons such as “one serving equals several prenatal capsules” without the exact products and a relevant clinical recommendation. A food's nutrient amount is not evidence that it replaces a supplement prescribed for your circumstances. The table is useful for understanding composition, while the weekly fish chart addresses a different question: how to choose seafood during pregnancy.

## Trimester-Specific Guidance

The [FDA/EPA seafood recommendation](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) and [CDC cooking precautions](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) apply to pregnancy generally. This page does not add a special salmon dose for a particular trimester or identify one trimester in which eating salmon guarantees the greatest benefit.

**First trimester:** Cooked salmon can fit the same guidance. If it is unappealing, this article does not prescribe forcing it into meals or selecting a supplement without individual advice.

**Second trimester:** Continue to count all seafood together in the weekly plan and check the actual preparation. The salmon entry is not an extra allowance on top of other fish.

**Third trimester:** Maintain the same food-safety precautions and the regional portion guidance. A nutrient's role in development is not permission to exceed the relevant fish recommendation or to increase a supplement dose yourself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is salmon safe to eat during pregnancy?**

Cooked salmon is a lower-mercury **Best Choice** on the [FDA/EPA chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish). Apply the overall seafood portions and [CDC cooking and smoked-fish precautions](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html). This is general guidance, not a guarantee about a particular meal.

**How much salmon per week is safe while pregnant?**

U.S. [FDA/EPA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) recommends 8–12 ounces of varied lower-mercury seafood weekly, with a 4-ounce serving guide and 2–3 Best Choice servings. Count salmon with your other seafood. U.K. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) limits total oily fish, including salmon, to two portions weekly. Local-catch advisories may impose further conditions.

**Can pregnant women eat cooked salmon?**

Yes, within the relevant seafood guidance. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) identifies fish cooked to 145°F or the opaque/easy-flaking endpoint as a safer choice. A browned exterior or a fixed recipe time alone does not establish the endpoint.

**Is raw salmon safe during pregnancy?**

[CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) recommends cooked fish instead of raw or undercooked fish, including sushi and sashimi. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) also advises against raw fish and raw-fish sushi, including previously frozen fish. Lower-mercury status does not address those preparation concerns.

**Can I eat salmon while pregnant in the first trimester?**

The same [FDA/EPA fish guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) and [CDC preparation precautions](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) apply. This page does not establish a separate first-trimester salmon allowance or a treatment for nausea.

**Is farmed salmon OK during pregnancy?**

The [FDA/EPA chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) lists salmon as a Best Choice without a separate allowance based on farming method. Check portions, preparation and any applicable advisories. [NIH food records](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) show that nutrient amounts differ by salmon preparation; do not infer a universal wild-versus-farmed ranking.

For more seafood questions, see our [pregnancy fish and seafood guide](/guides/fish-seafood-pregnancy). Check the specific species and preparation rather than treating all fish as equivalent.
