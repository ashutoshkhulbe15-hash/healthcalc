# Healthy Resting Heart Rate by Age: What's Normal and What's Not

---

## Introduction

> **Key Takeaway:** For most adults who are sitting or lying down, calm and feeling well, the American Heart Association describes a resting heart rate of 60–100 beats per minute. This is a reference range, not an individual diagnosis or a score in which the lowest number always wins. [AHA: All About Heart Rate](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

Your pulse is useful information, but it is only one part of a health assessment. A rate below 60 can occur in physically active people, during sleep or with medicines; a rate above 100 can be temporary or require assessment. The circumstances, symptoms and changes from your usual rate matter. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

This guide covers adult interpretation, a separately sourced age-reference table, measurement, possible influences and questions for a clinician. It does not diagnose a rhythm disorder from a pulse count, calculate life expectancy or prescribe a “perfect” resting rate.

**Emergency advice:** AHA advises calling 911 if your rate suddenly becomes very high or very low for you, especially with chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or fainting. Use the local emergency number outside the US. Do not wait for a value to cross a chart boundary before seeking help for serious symptoms. [AHA: When to call](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

## What Is Resting Heart Rate

Resting heart rate is the number of beats per minute while resting, rather than during exercise or immediately after it. AHA's adult reference specifies sitting or lying, calm and feeling well. MedlinePlus recommends resting for at least **10 minutes** before measuring a resting rate. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse); [MedlinePlus: Pulse](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

Do not confuse resting rate with an exercise target or with blood pressure. AHA explains that blood pressure measures the force of blood in the vessels, while pulse counts heartbeats. A resting pulse measurement does not tell you the blood-pressure reading. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

A lower rate can be associated with athletic conditioning, but it cannot by itself establish that the heart is stronger or healthier. AHA also lists medicines and possible medical causes of slow rates. Keep the meaning of the observation separate from an assumed explanation.

## Normal Resting Heart Rate

The table below reproduces **numerical reference intervals**, with age labels from MedlinePlus's pulse page, reviewed January 1, 2025. The athlete category is separate; it does not supply a different athletic cutoff for every age. These intervals are not emergency-triage rules or diagnostic criteria for an unwell child. [MedlinePlus: Normal results](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

| Age or group | Listed resting pulse reference, bpm |
|---|---|
| Newborn, 0–1 month | 70–190 |
| Infant, 1–11 months | 80–160 |
| Child, 1–2 years | 80–130 |
| Child, 3–4 years | 80–120 |
| Child, 5–6 years | 75–115 |
| Child, 7–9 years | 70–110 |
| Age 10 and older, including adults and seniors | 60–100 |
| Well-trained athletes, separate category | 40–60 |

Source: [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm). A baby's or child's assessment requires age and clinical context; do not substitute the adult 100-bpm boundary for pediatric interpretation. The table does not endorse 30–40 bpm as a goal for exceptional athletes or classify every active person with a low rate as healthy.

For adults, AHA uses 60–100 bpm as a normal resting range, while recognizing exceptions below 60. There is no separately verified “optimal 50–70” or “safest 60–80” range assigned here. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

A result in the table does not exclude disease if symptoms or an irregular rhythm are present. MedlinePlus advises discussing persistently high, below-reference or irregular pulses with a healthcare provider. [MedlinePlus: Abnormal results](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

## Factors That Affect

The following topics distinguish supported influences from claims that cannot be assigned to a person's pulse without assessment. Recording circumstances helps explain the observation; it does not prove a cause.

### Fitness Level

AHA says lower resting rates are common in athletes. It also explains that after exercise the rate does not return to resting level immediately. This does not establish a universal one-to-two-minute return time or a promised decrease of 5–10 bpm after eight weeks of walking. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

Record whether the measurement followed exercise, even if the activity felt light. Compare resting observations with resting observations rather than calling a post-workout value your baseline. Do not increase training solely to reach someone else's low pulse.

### Stress and Mental State

Stress, anxiety and other emotions can increase pulse; pain can also affect it. These are possible influences, not a diagnosis of anxiety whenever the heart rate rises. [AHA: Other factors](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

You can note stress alongside a reading and discuss it with your clinician. Our [stress information](/mental-health/stress-level-test) and [burnout information](/mental-health/burnout-quiz) do not determine the medical cause of a pulse change. This guide does not promise a fixed heart-rate reduction from meditation or diagnose a hormone response from a stressful week.

### Caffeine Intake

FDA lists increased heart rate and palpitations among symptoms of too much caffeine. Sensitivity and elimination vary, and products contain different amounts. That does not support a claim that every cup raises resting rate by 5–10 bpm for three to five hours. [FDA caffeine information](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much).

Record the product, amount and time if caffeine might be relevant. Include sources beyond coffee, such as energy drinks and supplements. Do not use a pulse reading to calculate a personal safe caffeine dose or assume a product is caffeine-free because it is labelled decaffeinated. [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much).

### Medications

AHA lists beta blockers, calcium-channel blockers and other medicines as possible reasons for a slower rate. NHS lists medicines among possible palpitation triggers and advises checking the leaflet. A medicine review belongs with the prescribing clinician. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse); [NHS palpitations](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/).

Keep the medicine name, dose and recent changes with your record. This page does not advise stopping a beta blocker, adjusting thyroid treatment or changing a stimulant prescription to alter the displayed number. Report unexpected changes and ask what monitoring plan applies to your treatment.

### Temperature and Environment

AHA notes that outside temperature can affect pulse and that an increase in body temperature may increase it. Position and recent activity also affect interpretation. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

Record unusual heat or a recent change from sitting to standing. AHA says pulse can rise briefly on standing and should settle after a few minutes. This guide does not require measurements at sea level or assign a fixed effect to cold exposure or altitude. Context is useful without converting every environmental difference into a medical rule.

### Dehydration

MedlinePlus lists dehydration as a possible explanation for a fast pulse. That does not establish a universal 2–3% fluid-loss threshold or a predictable bpm increase. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

A heart-rate number does not prescribe a water volume or an electrolyte supplement. If you have symptoms, illness or a medical fluid plan, discuss the circumstances with the clinical team rather than assuming that drinking more will resolve the problem.

### Illness and Infection

AHA lists fever and infection among possible causes of sinus tachycardia. MedlinePlus also says a fast pulse can signal infection. [AHA tachycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/tachycardia--fast-heart-rate); [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

An illness reading is still useful information. Label it as taken during illness instead of discarding it or waiting until recovery before seeking help. This guide does not promise a return to baseline within a set number of days; persistent changes and symptoms need the appropriate assessment.

### Hormonal Fluctuations

NHS notes that some people experience palpitations during pregnancy or menopause. Palpitations mean a noticeable or unusual heartbeat; they are not identical to a resting-heart-rate measurement. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/).

This guide does not assign a universal 2–5-bpm menstrual-cycle change or use that presumed change to declare symptoms harmless. If timing seems relevant, note it for the consultation while keeping medical assessment separate from an assumed hormonal explanation.

### Sleep Quality

NHS lists lack of sleep among common palpitation triggers. This supports noting poor sleep when discussing symptoms; it does not prove that a particular resting-rate change is due to sleep deprivation or that sleeping longer will lower it by a specified amount. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/).

Keep sleep observations distinct from diagnoses. A morning/evening difference is not enough to diagnose sleep apnea, and a normal pulse does not rule out a sleep disorder. Discuss ongoing sleep problems on their own merits rather than treating the pulse as a sleep test.

### Body Composition

AHA says people with obesity might have a higher heart rate than people without obesity. “Might” is not a guaranteed effect or a formula connecting pounds lost with bpm. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

This guide does not prescribe weight loss from a pulse reading, promise a 2–5-bpm decrease after losing five to ten pounds or infer body-fat percentage from heart rate. The [BMI calculator](/body-metrics/bmi-calculator) reports a separate screening measure, not the cause of a heart-rate change.

## When High Resting Heart Rate

### Tachycardia (RHR >100 bpm at rest)

AHA describes a resting adult rate over 100 bpm as tachycardia. The number describes speed; it does not identify the rhythm or establish the underlying cause. AHA lists several types and explains that assessment can involve an ECG and, for intermittent episodes, a portable monitor. [AHA tachycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/tachycardia--fast-heart-rate).

Possible causes of sinus tachycardia include anxiety, fever, pain, medicines, anemia, infection and increased thyroid activity, among others. This is a list of possibilities, not a way to diagnose yourself from accompanying symptoms. AHA emphasizes treating the cause rather than simply slowing the rate. [AHA: Sinus tachycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/tachycardia--fast-heart-rate).

Tell your healthcare professional if your pulse is persistently faster than usual or above the resting reference. A sudden very high or very low rate with concerning symptoms needs emergency help. [AHA: When to call](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

### Low Resting Heart Rate: When It's Normal vs. Concerning

AHA describes adult bradycardia as a rate below 60 bpm, with exceptions during sleep and in athletes or physically active adults. Below 40 is not the definition, and being above 40 does not establish that a slow rate is harmless. [AHA bradycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/bradycardia--slow-heart-rate).

Possible causes include problems with the sinus or AV node, low thyroid activity, some medicines and other conditions. Weakness, dizziness, fainting, breathlessness or chest pain can accompany a clinically important slow rate. Discuss the actual rate, symptoms and medical history rather than assuming athletic conditioning explains every low value. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/bradycardia--slow-heart-rate).

The retained disclaimer below mentions below 40 in non-athletes as a concern. It does not mean assessment should wait until 40, or that symptoms can be ignored in trained athletes. Emergency symptoms take priority over the reference chart.

## How to Improve Resting Heart Rate

The aim is to support health and address the cause of an abnormal rate, not to force the pulse to a preferred number. The following guidance does not promise a specified bpm change or replace evaluation of symptoms.

### Aerobic Exercise

AHA recommends adults work toward at least **150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly**, or **75 minutes of vigorous activity**, or a combination, plus muscle-strengthening activity at least **two days weekly**. Increase the amount and intensity gradually. People with a chronic condition or disability should discuss appropriate activity with their healthcare provider. [AHA activity recommendations](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/exercise-and-physical-activity/fitness-basics/aha-recs-for-physical-activity-in-adults).

Thirty minutes on five days is one arithmetic way to total 150 minutes, not a mandatory schedule or an eight-week pulse-lowering prescription. AHA also allows activity in shorter bouts. At moderate intensity breathing is harder but conversation remains possible; no personal exercise zone is prescribed from resting rate here. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/exercise-and-physical-activity/fitness-basics/aha-recs-for-physical-activity-in-adults).

### Stress Reduction

NHS says avoiding triggers such as stress can help palpitations when they are not caused by a health condition. That qualification matters. A relaxation routine is not a substitute for assessing unexplained or recurrent symptoms. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/).

You may choose a comfortable relaxation activity, but this guide gives no evidence-based minute dose that promises a particular bpm reduction. Do not use a pulse improvement after relaxation to rule out a medical cause.

### Improved Sleep

Keep a record of sleep problems and discuss persistent concerns with a clinician. Lack of sleep is a possible palpitation trigger in NHS guidance, but no fixed hours-to-bpm conversion is established here. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/).

This page does not prescribe a room temperature, universal afternoon caffeine cutoff or screen-free interval as a heart-rate treatment. Those choices should not become diagnostic rules or promises about the next morning's reading.

### Weight Management and Body Composition

Ask about an appropriate health plan if weight management is relevant. AHA's statement that obesity may affect pulse does not establish a personal weight target or prove that every rate change reflects body-fat change. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

### Limiting Stimulants

NHS includes caffeine, nicotine and alcohol among palpitation triggers. FDA recommends gradually reducing habitual caffeine when cutting back, because withdrawal can be unpleasant. Neither source promises a particular reduction in resting rate. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/heart-palpitations/); [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much).

### Hydration and Electrolytes

A fast pulse may occur with dehydration, but this guide does not prescribe eight to ten glasses or an electrolyte dose as treatment. The pulse does not establish a deficiency. Seek advice about illness or suspected dehydration and follow any existing clinical fluid instructions. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

## How to Measure

### Manual Pulse Check

1. Sit or lie quietly and rest for at least ten minutes before a resting measurement. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).
2. Place the index and middle fingers lightly on the inner wrist, below the thumb, until you feel the pulse. [AHA wrist technique](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).
3. Count for a full 60 seconds to obtain bpm. MedlinePlus also describes counting for 30 seconds and multiplying by two. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).
4. Record the value, time, position, method and any symptoms or relevant circumstances. A record supports discussion; it does not establish a diagnosis.

Use the wrist instructions here rather than pressing hard on the neck. MedlinePlus warns that neck pressure can cause fainting or slowing of the heartbeat, and that both sides should not be pressed simultaneously. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).

### Heart Rate Monitors and Devices

A device readout is a measurement from that device, not an ECG diagnosis. This guide does not rank every chest strap, watch, oximeter or phone app by a universal accuracy percentage. Follow the specific instructions and report repeated unexpected readings or symptoms to your clinical team.

An intermittent rhythm problem may need a clinical recording even when an office ECG appears normal. AHA describes Holter or event monitoring for some intermittent fast rhythms; that is different from claiming a consumer device can confirm or exclude the condition. [AHA tachycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/tachycardia--fast-heart-rate).

### Conditions for Accurate Measurement

Identify whether the reading was taken at rest, after activity, during illness or after standing. Use comparable conditions when you want to compare resting values. The reference sources do not establish a universal three-to-seven-day diagnostic averaging period or require discarding weekends and stressful days.

**Illustrative seven-day log — invented observations, not clinical evidence:**

| Day | Recorded bpm | Circumstances to record |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 72 | Resting measurement; method noted |
| Tuesday | 75 | Sleep and caffeine timing noted |
| Wednesday | 68 | Same method; symptoms noted |
| Thursday | 71 | Recent activity recorded |
| Friday | 78 | Illness or medication changes recorded |
| Saturday | 66 | Different routine recorded |
| Sunday | 70 | Same units; collection time recorded |

The arithmetic average is 500 ÷ 7, approximately **71.4 bpm**. That calculation does not show that sleep, caffeine or stress caused any difference, and averaging cannot make a concerning episode harmless. Bring individual readings and symptoms to the appointment rather than reporting only the average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is a lower resting heart rate always better?**

A: No. A low rate can reflect physical conditioning or medicines, but it can also require assessment. AHA's usual adult resting range is 60–100, with exceptions below 60. Symptoms and context matter; this guide assigns no optimal 50–70 target. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

**Q: How long does it take for aerobic exercise to lower RHR?**

A: No universal timeline or amount is promised here. AHA supports regular physical activity for health, but its weekly activity recommendation does not guarantee a 5–10-bpm decrease in four, eight or twelve weeks. Work toward appropriate activity and discuss persistent pulse changes separately. [AHA activity recommendations](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/exercise-and-physical-activity/fitness-basics/aha-recs-for-physical-activity-in-adults).

**Q: Why is my RHR higher in the morning than evening?**

A: Timing alone cannot identify the cause. Check whether both readings were taken at rest under comparable conditions and record symptoms, medicines and circumstances. AHA lists several influences, but this pattern does not diagnose sleep apnea. Discuss persistent unexpected changes. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

**Q: Can resting heart rate predict longevity?**

A: A pulse cannot calculate an individual's life expectancy. An older AHA News article describes an association in a specific Danish study of men; it is not an official clinical risk calculator or proof that lowering the rate extends life. This guide does not apply that study's relative-risk percentage to every reader. [AHA News, February 2021](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2021/02/10/watch-your-heart-rate-but-dont-obsess-about-it). The article itself notes its age and distinguishes news reports from official guidance.

**Q: Should I measure RHR on weekends if my schedule varies?**

A: Follow any monitoring schedule your clinician gives you. If you choose to keep a log, record the different circumstances rather than selecting only reassuring readings. AHA specifically advises people asked to monitor pulse while taking a beta blocker to ask when and how often to track it. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).

## Key Takeaways

- The usual adult resting reference is 60–100 bpm, with context and exceptions. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).
- Pediatric reference intervals are age-specific; the table is not a diagnosis for an unwell child. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm).
- Below 60 is the usual adult bradycardia definition, not below 40. [AHA bradycardia](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia/about-arrhythmia/bradycardia--slow-heart-rate).
- Symptoms and unexpected changes deserve assessment; serious symptoms require emergency help. [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).
- Activity recommendations support health without guaranteeing a pulse reduction. [AHA activity](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/exercise-and-physical-activity/fitness-basics/aha-recs-for-physical-activity-in-adults).
- A pulse log, BMI, stress information and a sleep diary do not combine into a complete diagnosis.

## Medical Disclaimer

This article is for educational purposes only and is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Resting heart rate monitoring is a health assessment tool, not a diagnostic test.

If your RHR is persistently elevated (>100 bpm), unusually low (<40 bpm in non-athletes), or accompanied by symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, fainting, or palpitations, consult a licensed healthcare provider immediately.

Individuals with known cardiac conditions, arrhythmias, or those taking heart-rate-affecting medications should have their RHR evaluated by a healthcare professional rather than relying on self-measurement alone. Do not use this article as a basis for stopping or changing medications without medical guidance.

**Clarification:** The disclaimer's below-40 example is not the definition of bradycardia and is not a threshold to wait for. Sudden abnormal rates with serious symptoms need emergency care as explained above. [AHA emergency advice](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure/all-about-heart-rate-pulse).
