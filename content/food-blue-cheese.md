# Can I Eat Blue Cheese During Pregnancy? Pasteurization Guide

**Quick answer:** Do not use pasteurization alone to decide whether to eat cold blue cheese during pregnancy. U.K. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) requires thorough cooking for soft blue-veined cheese made with either pasteurized or raw milk. U.S. [CDC pregnancy guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) identifies pasteurized soft cheese heated to 165°F or until steaming hot as a safer choice. The recommendations are about the actual cheese and its preparation, not a promise that a supermarket brand is free of contamination.

Blue cheese questions often mix several separate issues: whether the milk was pasteurized, the type of finished cheese, whether it is eaten cold or cooked, and which country's advice applies. A cheese name or a photograph cannot answer all four. Below is a practical way to check the product without assuming that a familiar brand, imported origin or melted appearance guarantees safety.

## Why Blue Cheese Is Risky: Listeria and Mold

The concern relevant to pregnancy is contamination with **Listeria**, rather than an assumption that every visible blue vein means the cheese is spoiled. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html) identifies pregnancy as a circumstance in which infection can be particularly harmful. Its food guidance distinguishes higher-risk cheese choices from safer alternatives. That distinction is more useful than calling all cheese dangerous or treating all pasteurized cheese as risk-free.

Milk treatment is only one part of the assessment. [CDC's pregnancy food chart](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) includes precautions for certain finished cheeses even when made with pasteurized milk, including queso fresco-type cheeses and deli-sliced cheese. This is why “pasteurized” should not be treated as a universal clearance for every cheese product or every way of serving it.

Infection during pregnancy can lead to pregnancy loss, premature delivery or serious newborn infection. The [CDC symptom guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/signs-symptoms/index.html) also explains that the pregnant person's symptoms can be mild or absent. These are consequences of infection, not the chance that a serving of cheese will cause them. This article does not assign an individual miscarriage percentage to eating blue cheese or calculate your risk from a particular meal.

## Pasteurized vs. Unpasteurized Blue Cheese: How to Tell

Start with the package's ingredient information and any explicit statement about pasteurized milk. If a restaurant serves the cheese, ask whether staff can check the packaging for the actual product used. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) recommends checking packaging for pasteurization, but its soft-blue-cheese restriction still applies even when the milk is pasteurized.

Do not infer milk treatment from a country, a protected name, a supermarket chain or a price. This page does not certify the formulation of any brand. A previous purchase also does not establish the ingredients of a different package or a replacement used by a restaurant.

| What you know about the cheese | What still needs checking |
|---|---|
| The label explicitly says pasteurized milk | Whether the cheese type still requires cooking under the guidance you follow |
| The label says raw or unpasteurized milk | Apply the raw-milk-cheese precaution; do not eat it cold on the basis of a brand name |
| The cheese is supplied as loose crumbles | Request the original product information; crumbled form does not establish milk treatment |
| The menu says blue cheese dressing | Check the cheese ingredient and how the dressing is prepared and served |
| The cheese is on a hot dish | Establish that the cheese itself was cooked through rather than sprinkled on afterwards |
| The product information is unavailable | Choose an identifiable alternative rather than guessing its ingredients |

The raw-milk and heating precautions above follow [CDC's cheese guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html) and its [pregnancy food chart](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html). The table is a checking framework, not a laboratory assessment of a product.

**Decision sequence:** Identify the cheese; read its milk-treatment information; apply the cheese-specific guidance for your country; then check how it is served. If the relevant information is missing, the result is “not established,” rather than “safe because it probably uses pasteurized milk.”

## Cooking Blue Cheese: Source-Specific Heating Advice

For U.S. pregnancy guidance, [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) lists pasteurized soft cheeses heated to **165°F or until steaming hot** among safer choices. Its [Listeria prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html) also uses that endpoint for raw-milk cheeses. The **165°F** value is the cited U.S. endpoint; this article does not replace it with an unsupported universal 158°F rule.

For U.K. readers, the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) permits cheese cooked until steaming hot and specifically requires thorough cooking of soft blue-veined cheeses. It does not give a 158°F cheese threshold on that page. Follow the guidance applicable to you rather than combining numbers from unrelated cooking instructions.

“Cooked in the dish” and “added to the hot dish” are different preparations. A pizza, baked potato, burger or pasta sauce does not establish the cheese's heating endpoint simply because the surrounding food is hot. Likewise, melted texture alone is not the CDC's stated endpoint. Ask how the cheese was prepared if someone else cooked the meal.

Examples of questions to ask are: Was the cheese baked with the pizza or added afterwards? Was the sauce heated after the cheese was mixed in? Were cold crumbles placed on the plate just before serving? These questions apply the [CDC heating advice](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html); they do not guarantee safety for a recipe based on its name or a fixed number of minutes.

Use an appropriate food thermometer when checking a numerical endpoint. A cooking time printed for a different quantity, thickness or appliance is not a measurement of your dish. For a mixed recipe, consider the requirements for its other ingredients as well; a cheese rule does not replace instructions for meat, eggs or leftovers.

## Common Blue Cheese Products by Safety Status

A useful product comparison is based on verifiable information rather than a permanent list of “approved” brands. The same style of cheese may be sold in different preparations, and a restaurant dressing may not use the product you expect. Choose the row that describes what you can actually establish.

| Product or preparation | Pregnancy guidance and practical action |
|---|---|
| Cold soft blue cheese, labelled pasteurized | U.K.: still requires thorough cooking under NHS guidance. U.S.: use CDC's heated-soft-cheese safer option rather than treating pasteurization as a guarantee |
| Raw-milk blue cheese | CDC advises avoiding raw-milk cheese unless heated to its specified endpoint |
| Blue cheese cooked throughout a dish | Check the relevant heating endpoint for the cheese and the other ingredients |
| Cold restaurant dressing or dip | A menu name is insufficient; ask about ingredients and preparation, or choose an alternative with clearer information |
| Cheese sprinkled onto an already hot meal | Do not assume the topping reached the heating endpoint |
| Package implicated in a recall | Follow the recall instructions rather than trying to decide from its appearance |

The cheese rows reflect [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) and [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) guidance. Consult [FDA recall notices](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts) for a product subject to a U.S. alert. This page does not assert that any named brand is currently recalled or certify that other batches are uncontaminated.

For an alternative, CDC specifically lists pasteurized hard cheeses and pasteurized cream cheese, cottage cheese, string cheese, feta and mozzarella as safer choices in its [Listeria prevention chart](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html). “Safer” is the comparison used by the guidance, not a guarantee about an individual package.

## Nutritional Value of Blue Cheese

Use the Nutrition Facts label for the cheese you actually buy. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/whats-nutrition-facts-label) explains that amounts generally apply to the stated serving and that a labelled serving size is not a recommendation about how much to eat. A generic “one ounce of blue cheese” table cannot certify every product's calcium, protein, sodium or saturated-fat content.

| Information to read | How to use it |
|---|---|
| Serving weight | Compare products on the same amount rather than on differently sized servings |
| Calcium and protein | Read the stated amount; do not substitute an estimate from a different cheese |
| Sodium and saturated fat | Compare the actual values alongside the other ingredients in the meal |
| Percent Daily Value | Use it as label context, not as an individualized pregnancy prescription |

These comparisons follow the [FDA label guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/whats-nutrition-facts-label). **Illustrative arithmetic only:** if a hypothetical label gives 120 mg of calcium per 30 g, 15 g would supply approximately 60 mg. The calculation halves the labelled quantity; it is not a measured value for blue cheese or a recommendation to eat that portion.

Nutrition does not cancel the preparation guidance. You can assess a product's nutrient contribution and still choose a different cheese because you cannot establish the milk treatment or cooking method. There is no need to claim that blue cheese is essential for fetal development or that a substitute has identical nutrition without checking its own label.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Can blue cheese cause miscarriage?

[Listeria infection during pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/signs-symptoms/index.html) can cause pregnancy loss and other serious outcomes. That does not mean eating blue cheese proves infection or predicts a miscarriage. Apply the [cheese-specific prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html), and ask your healthcare provider about a concerning exposure or symptoms rather than assigning yourself a percentage from a food article.

Is blue cheese dressing safe during pregnancy?

A brand name, bottle or preservative claim does not establish pregnancy suitability. Check the actual ingredients and preparation. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) requires cooking soft blue-veined cheese even when pasteurized; [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) identifies heated pasteurized soft cheese as a safer choice. Cold dressing is not the same preparation as cheese cooked throughout a dish.

What other soft cheeses should I avoid during pregnancy?

[CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html) advises avoiding unheated queso fresco-type cheese whether the milk is pasteurized or raw, and avoiding raw-milk cheese unless appropriately heated. U.K. [NHS advice](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) additionally requires thorough cooking of white-rind soft-ripened cheeses. Do not use “pasteurized” as an exception to every cheese restriction.

I ate blue cheese before I knew I was pregnant. What should I do?

Eating it does not diagnose infection. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/signs-symptoms/index.html) advises contacting a healthcare provider after recalled or outbreak-linked food if you develop fever and other Listeria symptoms, and telling them about possible exposure, especially in pregnancy. If you are not ill, CDC says most experts do not think tests or treatment are needed; discuss questions with your provider. Mild or absent symptoms are possible, so this article does not offer a fixed symptom-free deadline as a guarantee.
