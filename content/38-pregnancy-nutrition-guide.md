# Pregnancy Nutrition: Reference Intakes and Practical Limits

Nutrition advice should distinguish a total daily reference intake from a supplement dose. A prenatal product is not automatically a complete supply of every nutrient, and taking several products can duplicate ingredients.

## Selected US daily reference intakes

[NIH Office of Dietary Supplements' pregnancy fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) summarizes these values:

| Nutrient | Pregnancy reference | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Folate | 600 mcg DFE/day | DFE is not the same unit as mcg folic acid |
| Iron | 27 mg/day | Total reference intake; treatment doses differ |
| Calcium | 1,000 mg/day for ages 19–50; 1,300 for ages 14–18 | Requirement varies by age |
| Vitamin D | 600 IU/day (15 mcg) | Total intake, not a universal deficiency-treatment dose |
| Choline | 450 mg/day | Adequate intake; check whether a prenatal contains it |

This is a selected reference table, not a complete nutrition prescription. Food choices, dietary restrictions, medical conditions and product ingredients affect an individual plan. A universal DHA supplement dose is not assigned here.

## Folate versus folic acid

[NIH's folate guidance](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/) distinguishes pregnancy's 600 mcg DFE reference from advice for folic acid supplementation before conception and early pregnancy. The neural tube closes very early, around 21–28 days after conception, rather than at week 12. Individual higher-dose instructions should come from your care team.

## Energy needs are estimates

[CDC’s US guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-weight/index.html) describes approximately 340 additional calories in the second trimester and 450 in the third. These are general guidance, not a compulsory addition regardless of baseline intake, activity or pregnancy circumstances. Other national guidance can use different population assumptions.

## Reading a supplement label

Write down the serving size and amount per serving for every product you use. If one contains 15 mcg vitamin D and another 25 mcg, the sum is 40 mcg, equivalent to 1,600 IU. That example demonstrates addition and unit conversion; it is not a recommended dose. The [NIH vitamin D section](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) explains the 1 mcg = 40 IU conversion. Check combined products with your care team.

## Food handling matters too

Follow [FDA pregnancy food-safety guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/food-safety-pregnant-women-and-their-unborn-babies) for preparation, pasteurization and storage. A nutrient-rich food can still need cooking or special handling. The [food checker](/pregnancy/safe-food-checker) summarizes common preparation concerns and links the relevant advice.
