# Pregnancy Nutrition Guide: Intake References, Supplements and Food Safety

Pregnancy nutrition involves both food choices and the meaning of nutrient recommendations. A **total daily intake** is not the same as a supplement dose. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements distinguishes the nutrient's form, age group, pregnancy context and the amounts supplied by foods and supplements. [NIH pregnancy reference](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/)

Not every nutrient's reference increases in pregnancy: for example, calcium's age-specific RDA is unchanged from that for nonpregnant females of the same age. Nor does a product called “prenatal” necessarily contain everything needed. [NIH calcium and prenatal-supplement sections](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/)

## The Key Nutrients During Pregnancy

> **Key Takeaway:** Read the units and population beside each amount. Do not treat a total-intake reference as an instruction to take that amount in a pill.

| Nutrient | U.S. pregnancy intake reference | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Folate | 600 mcg DFE/day for ages 14–50 | DFE means dietary folate equivalents; not 600 mcg of supplemental folic acid |
| Iron | 27 mg/day for ages 14–50 | An intake RDA, not an anemia-treatment dose |
| Calcium | 1,300 mg/day at ages 14–18; 1,000 at 19–50 | Age-specific and unchanged from nonpregnancy in those groups |
| Vitamin D | 15 mcg/day, equivalent to 600 IU, at ages 14–50 | Total intake; not a deficiency-treatment dose |
| DHA | No DRI amount specified as DHA in the table | Do not label an unverified 200–300 mg rule as a universal RDA |

[NIH Table 1 and nutrient sections](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/)

The previous protein figure and “six most important nutrients” ranking are not used as a complete nutrition prescription. NIH also discusses iodine, choline and other nutrients. The current U.S. dietary guideline highlights iron, folate and iodine in pregnancy and advises discussing a daily prenatal vitamin with a healthcare professional. [U.S. guideline, pregnancy section](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf)

For any supplement, compare the actual ingredient forms, amounts and serving size with your professional advice. Taking several products can add overlapping ingredients. A source table cannot establish that your particular combination is appropriate.

## Trimester-Specific Nutrition

CDC's U.S. guidance says **in general** no additional calories are required in the first trimester, with about **340 extra kcal/day** in the second and **450** in the third. These are typical additions, not a total daily intake or an individualized assessment. [CDC caloric-needs paragraph](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-weight/index.html)

The earlier precise week-by-week nutrient story included unverified closure timing, peak calcium deposition and universal snack equivalents. Those figures are not retained. A specific meal's energy depends on its actual foods and portions; saying “one snack” does not verify 340 kcal.

**Arithmetic illustration:** If an assumed baseline were 2,000 kcal, adding 340 would give 2,340. This is an addition example, not a recommendation that 2,000 or 2,340 is right for you. Twin pregnancy, illness or other circumstances should not be handled by blindly applying the same website arithmetic.

If nausea or vomiting limits intake, tell your prenatal provider rather than assuming “survival eating” makes a continuing problem harmless. Keep advice about symptom management separate from the reference nutrient table.

## Foods to Avoid During Pregnancy

The following is **U.S. food-safety guidance**, not a universal statement about every country's regulated food products:

| Food or handling issue | Verified advice |
|---|---|
| Raw or undercooked fish | CDC lists it as riskier; cooked fish can be prepared to 145°F or the stated visual cooking endpoint |
| Raw milk and dairy | Choose pasteurized products and check the specific cheese guidance |
| Deli meats and hot dogs | Heat to 165°F or until steaming hot |
| Eggs | Cook yolks and whites firm; use pasteurized eggs for preparations not cooked safely |
| Higher-mercury fish | Consult FDA's species-specific “Choices to Avoid” list |
| Alcohol | CDC states there is no known safe amount or safe time during pregnancy |

[CDC safer-food table](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html); [FDA fish advice](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish); [CDC alcohol guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/fasd/about/index.html)

Pasteurization is not a blanket rule that every cheese can be eaten unheated: CDC's current table gives particular cautions for queso-fresco-type and deli-sliced cheeses. Read the exact product category rather than collapsing the guidance into “all pasteurized cheese is safe.” [CDC cheese rows](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html)

FDA advises pregnant or breastfeeding people to eat two to three four-ounce servings weekly from its lower-mercury **Best Choices**, or one serving from **Good Choices**. Species and serving context matter. [FDA chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish)

Our [food guidance directory](/pregnancy/safe-food-checker) links to references. Individual food articles still require their own review; the directory does not verify every article or guarantee that a product is safe.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many extra calories do I need during pregnancy?**

CDC describes the typical additions above: generally none in the first trimester, around 340 in the second and 450 in the third. They are not your total requirement or a fixed snack prescription. Ask your provider how they relate to your circumstances. [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-weight/index.html)

**What should I eat in the first trimester?**

Use a varied food pattern and your prenatal provider's advice, while following the relevant food-safety guidance. The U.S. guideline advises discussing a daily prenatal vitamin with a healthcare professional. This guide does not assume that persistent vomiting or restricted intake can be ignored. [U.S. pregnancy guidance](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf); [CDC food safety](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html)

**Do I need a prenatal vitamin?**

The current U.S. guideline advises talking to a healthcare professional about taking a daily prenatal vitamin. NIH explains that formulations vary and some lack particular nutrients. Do not assume a prenatal product guarantees adequate DHA, calcium, iodine or every other listed nutrient, or that extra supplements are automatically needed. [Guideline](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf); [NIH formulation discussion](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/)
